Snap robots

Pick a song, tap Go, and watch the two robots play along.

What you’re looking at (straight talk)

The picture: You picked a real audio file on your device. The page plays it in the browser, listens to its loudness and tone in real time, and uses that signal to move two small robot figures in front — arms, heads, bounce, legs — while a simple 3D “warehouse” behind them breathes with the same beat. No app store, no login, no server required for the demo itself.

What’s actually being demonstrated: Sound in → numbers out → motion and lighting out. That pipeline is the same idea behind music games, concert visuals, motion-capture previews, and future machines that need to “feel” rhythm without a person wiring every frame by hand. Here it’s proven in one lightweight web page.

What’s a little new about it: Most people only see either a flashy visualizer or a heavy game. This is the middle path: readable characters plus a real space, both driven by the same live audio analysis, shipped as static files anyone can open. That’s unusually portable for how much is going on.

Put the achievement in perspective: Big studios spend piles of money on toolchains to do versions of this. You’re looking at a pocket-sized slice — not Hollywood — but the stack is honest: decode audio, analyze spectrum, choreograph SVG, render WebGL, keep it smooth on a normal phone or laptop. That’s the hard part boiled down to something you can fork and build on.

Why it matters for people: When listening can steer motion and space in real time, you open doors for dance teaching aids, accessible “feel the music” feedback, rehearsal rigs, kid-friendly instruments, and eventually helper robots that move with human timing instead of random twitching. The catch for mankind is the same as always: tools like this should amplify human choice and creativity — who controls the signal, who gets credit, who stays in the loop — not replace the person on stage.
